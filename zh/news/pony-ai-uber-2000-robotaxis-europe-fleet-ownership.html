Pony.ai and Uber put 2,000 robotaxis on Europe's table — and hand the keys to someone elsePony.ai and Uber put 2,000 robotaxis on Europe's table — and hand the keys to someone else

The expanded Pony.ai-Uber agreement covers more than 2,000 autonomous vehicles across five European cities, building on the Zagreb service launched with Rimac-backed Verne on April 8, 2026. No city names, no timeline — but the structure of the deal, in which local partners own and finance the fleets, says more about how Chinese autonomy intends to enter Europe than the headline number does.

By TopChinaCar Editorial作者：TopChinaCar 编辑部 · Published August 16, 2026发布于 2026-08-16 · Compiled from the primary sources cited below基于文末所列一手信源编写

Pony.ai and Uber say they intend to put more than 2,000 robotaxis on the road across five European cities — Zagreb plus four others they have not named — under an expanded partnership that also includes plans to explore deployments in the Middle East. There is no disclosed timeline for reaching that fleet size, and no disclosed geography beyond the Croatian capital.

The base they are building from is small and precisely defined. Pony.ai launched Europe's first commercial robotaxi service in Zagreb on April 8, 2026, with local partner Verne and Uber, covering roughly 90 square kilometres of the city centre and airport area and operating daily from 7:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m. The vehicles are BAIC Arcfox Alpha T5 electric SUVs running Pony.ai's seventh-generation autonomous driving system. Verne — the mobility company founded by Mate Rimac — owns and operates them. The service is soon to be integrated into the Uber app.

The division of labour is the story. Pony.ai supplies SAE Level 4-certified autonomous driving technology. Uber supplies booking, payment and customer service. Local fleet operators handle day-to-day operations, financing and vehicle ownership, depending on the market. Pony.ai founder and CEO James Peng called the expanded agreement an important new phase in the partnership; Uber's Global Head of Autonomous Mobility & Delivery, Sarfraz Maredia, framed it as a move away from isolated individual launches toward scalable, repeatable commercial deployment.

That framing is accurate, and it is also where the hard part starts.

Anyone who has sat on the supplier side of an OEM relationship will recognise what Pony.ai is doing here: it is behaving like a Tier 1. It ships the stack, validates it on a platform vehicle, and lets the customer carry the capital and the operating headache. In the Zagreb configuration, the asset risk sits with Verne — the depot, the charging, the cleaning, the technicians, the local labour arrangements, the municipal relationships. Pony.ai's exposure is technical performance and integration. Uber's exposure is demand and the app layer. It is an efficient structure, and it is the reason a Chinese autonomy company reached commercial revenue in a European capital before most Western AV developers reached one.

It is also the constraint nobody in the announcement has to talk about yet. Multiplying that structure by four means finding four more Vernes — partners with balance sheets willing to own hundreds of autonomous EVs, and with the local standing to run them in a European city. Europe does not have a deep bench of Rimac-adjacent mobility operators. The slow variables in an overseas rollout are never the software integration; they are the ops organisation, the compliance posture and the government relations, and in this model Pony.ai has outsourced exactly those to a partner class that has to be recruited city by city. Concrete gets poured fast. Depot managers and remote-assistance rosters do not.

The arithmetic underlines how large a step this is. Zagreb's launch was part of Pony.ai's stated goal of 3,000 operational vehicles globally by the end of 2026. More than 2,000 vehicles in five European cities alone would be two-thirds of that global target concentrated in one region — an average of roughly 400 per city, against a Zagreb service currently confined to about 90 square kilometres and a 14-hour operating day. The absence of a timeline is not an oversight. The 2,000 figure is plainly not a 2026 number, and the four unnamed cities are the variable that determines whether it is a 2027 number or a 2029 one.

What the deal does establish is a route into Western European mobility that avoids the front door. Chinese autonomy software and a Chinese-built EV — BAIC's Arcfox — are already carrying paying passengers in an EU capital, arriving not as an import brand fighting for dealer space but as a technology and hardware layer inside a locally owned fleet booked through a US ride-hailing app. Pony.ai already runs fully driverless, paid services in several major Chinese cities, which is the operational track record that makes it credible to a partner like Verne in the first place. Baidu's Apollo Go and WeRide are pursuing the same overseas logic as domestic competition intensifies; Pony.ai has simply found the cleanest commercial wrapper for it so far.

The open question is regulatory appetite. Europe has tolerated a geofenced pilot in Zagreb. A commitment on the order of 2,000 Chinese-technology autonomous vehicles across five cities is a different proposition for regulators, and it will be tested one city approval at a time.

Watch the four names when they come. The choice of cities will reveal which local operators Pony.ai and Uber have actually signed — and in this structure, the operator matters more than the market.
